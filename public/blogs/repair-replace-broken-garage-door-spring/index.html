Repair or Replace a Broken Garage Door Spring

By BSD Garage Door Editorial Team  ·  4 min read

Start here. If a garage door spring breaks, you must choose between repairing the broken component or replacing spring(s) and related parts. Your safest initial move is to stop using the door and assess these three things: which spring type you have, whether the other spring shows similar wear, and whether there is additional hardware damage. This article walks you through the practical signs, a simple decision guide, cost factors that affect your choice, and safe next steps you can take while you arrange service.

TL;DR

  • A single broken spring can sometimes be replaced alone, but paired springs usually should be replaced together.
  • Torsion spring work is high risk; hire a trained technician if you are not equipped and experienced.
  • Compare quotes based on spring cycle rating and whether related parts are included.
  • Stop using the door as soon as you suspect a spring problem to avoid injury or further damage.
Immediate safetyA broken spring makes the door heavy and unpredictable; do not try to open it manually.
Typical optionsRepair a section or replace the full spring assembly depending on wear and type.
Torsion vs extensionTorsion springs last longer but are more dangerous to service without tools and training.

Should you repair or replace the spring

Use these conditions to choose the safer, more cost-effective option for your situation.

Single break on one spring and the other spring is recentRepair or replace only the broken spring
If the paired spring is relatively new and shows low wear, replacing the single broken spring can restore balance at lower cost.
Both springs are old, show heavy rust, or are past expected cyclesReplace both springs
Springs are paired and age similarly. Replacing both reduces the chance of a near-term repeat failure and improves door balance.
Torsion system with visible shaft or hardware damageReplace the full assembly
Damage to drums, shaft, or anchors can compromise safety and cause repeated breakdowns if only springs are swapped.
Commercial door or heavy-duty usageInstall heavier-duty springs or commercial-rated assembly
Frequent cycles wear springs faster; upgrading to higher-cycle springs reduces downtime and repair frequency.

How springs differ and why that matters

There are two common spring systems on residential and commercial doors: torsion springs and extension springs.

  • Torsion springs sit on a shaft above the door and wind to lift the door. They usually require more skill, special tools, and safety precautions to replace.
  • Extension springs run along the tracks and stretch to lift. They are simpler but still store dangerous energy.

Why it matters: the type determines the risk of attempting a DIY fix, the likely cost, and the benefit of replacing springs in pairs to keep the door balanced.

Signs that point toward replacement instead of repair

Replace springs rather than only repairing when you see any of the following:

  • Heavy rust, pitting, or multiple visible breaks on the spring surface.
  • Both springs are near the end of their expected life or installed at the same time many years ago.
  • Damage to related hardware such as drums, cables, or the torsion shaft.
  • The door serves a commercial or high-cycle application and will see frequent use.

If you find these conditions, replacing both springs and worn hardware reduces the chance of a repeat failure and improves door balance.

Factors that influence cost

These details determine whether a repair or replacement will cost more. Use them when comparing quotes.

Spring type

Torsion springs are pricier and more complex to replace than extension springs.

Door weight and size

Heavier doors require higher-rated springs, which cost more and may require additional labor.

Age and condition of related parts

Worn cables, drums, rollers, or tracks often should be replaced at the same time to avoid repeat service calls.

Labor and access

Difficult access or commercial installations increase labor time and cost.

Warranty and spring cycle rating

Higher-cycle springs cost more up front but can lower lifetime repair expenses.

A safe, practical repair route

If only one spring is broken and its partner is relatively new with low wear, replacing the single spring can be sensible. Follow these practical steps while you wait for service:

  • Disconnect the opener so the door cannot move unexpectedly.
  • Keep the door closed and secured; a closed door is safer than a partially open, unbalanced one.
  • Do not attempt to wind or unwind torsion springs without proper equipment and training.
  • Note the spring type, any markings, and approximate age for the technician. This information speeds diagnosis and keeps quotes accurate.

After you gather these facts, professional replacement will restore balance and function safely.

When to call a pro and what to expect

Spring work is hazardous. A trained technician will inspect the door, measure spring size and cycle rating, check cables, drums, rollers, and the opener connection, and recommend either a single-spring replacement or a paired replacement with any needed hardware changes. If you want a stronger long-term solution, ask about higher-cycle springs or heavy-duty commercial options if your door sees frequent use.

Signs a broken spring needs professional attention

If you notice any of the following, stop using the door and get help.

  • Door will not open or is extremely heavy
    Do not force the opener or try to lift the door manually; consult a technician.
  • Spring is visibly snapped or hanging loose
    Keep clear of the door path and secure pets and children; arrange service.
  • Loud bang when operating the door
    This is often a spring failure; avoid operating the door until inspected.
  • Door opens crooked or only partway
    This indicates imbalance from spring trouble or cable issues; professional balance is required.

Costs and tradeoffs to expect

Expect quotes to vary based on spring type, door size and weight, and whether related parts need replacement. The major tradeoffs are short-term savings versus long-term reliability. Replacing only a single broken spring costs less initially but can lead to another failure if the partner is near the end of its life. Upgrading to higher-cycle springs increases upfront cost but reduces downtime and future service calls. Use the cost factors module above to compare estimates with this in mind.

Final recommendations

If you are unsure, prioritize safety: stop using the door and get a professional inspection. For most homeowners, replacing both springs when one fails is a practical long-term choice unless the other spring is demonstrably new. For commercial doors or heavy use, choose springs rated for higher cycles. Keep a record of the work and the spring cycle rating for future decisions.

Frequently asked questions

Can I open the garage door manually if a spring is broken?

You can open a door manually only if it is safe to do so and the door is balanced. A broken spring makes the door very heavy and unpredictable. Avoid lifting it by hand and do not operate the opener. Instead, secure the area and contact a professional.

How long do garage door springs usually last?

Springs are rated by cycles. A common residential spring might be rated for 10,000 cycles, which can be several years depending on daily use. Higher-cycle springs last longer but cost more.

Is it necessary to replace cables or rollers when I replace springs?

Not always, but it is often recommended. Cables and rollers wear alongside springs. Replacing worn components at the same time reduces the chance of another failure and improves door function.

What is the difference between repairing a spring and installing a new door?

Repairing or replacing springs addresses lifting mechanics only. New door installation replaces the entire door and hardware and is appropriate when the door is damaged, inefficient, or when you want a different configuration or upgraded components.

How should I choose between a repair technician and an installer?

Choose a technician experienced with spring systems and the door type you have. For commercial doors or heavy-duty systems, look for specialists who document spring cycle ratings and inspect related hardware before recommending a repair or replacement.
